How this works

NISAR's L-band radar sends microwave pulses that pass through clouds and work day and night, which matters in a monsoon when optical satellites see only cloud. Calm water acts like a mirror and bounces the pulse away from the satellite, so flooded land turns very dark in HH (horizontal send / horizontal receive) backscatter. Rice paddies, forest and towns stay bright, and the cross-polarised HV channel helps separate vegetation from bare wet soil.

For each NISAR pass, the pipeline reads the L2 GCOV backscatter over the study area, converts it to decibels and applies one water threshold to every date, set at the valley between the dark water peak and the brighter land values in the HH histogram. It then counts flooded area, compares each date with the first pass of the season and builds a map of how often each pixel was under water.

    Limits

    Wind-roughened water and flooded vegetation can look bright and be missed. Very smooth sand bars and radar shadow can look dark and be falsely labelled as water. Treat the numbers as estimates, not official flood statistics.
